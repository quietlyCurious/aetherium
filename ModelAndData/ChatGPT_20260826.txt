Yes — and I think there’s a really interesting opportunity here to define what an operator interface becomes when it no longer has to be primarily a collection of screens.

Your current plan is strong. I’d turn it into four phases:

Understand the operator’s world
Model the decisions and operating modes
Define the role of AI
Design the next-generation interaction model

That middle step is important. If we jump directly from “operators do these tasks” to “how could AI help?”, we risk creating a traditional HMI/MES experience with a chatbot bolted onto it. The more interesting question is: what information, decisions, actions, and coordination does the operator actually need at each moment—and how should the system dynamically organize itself around that?

That direction also lines up nicely with current thinking in the field. ISA-101 continues to emphasize operator awareness, usability, navigation, graphics and alarm presentation, while NIST’s manufacturing work is explicitly looking at productive human/machine teaming and “augmented intelligence,” rather than simply replacing the human with automation.

1. Understand the operator’s world

I would resist starting with screens or even features. Start with a day-in-the-life / shift-in-the-life model.

And because you're covering both automation operators and manufacturing operators, I’d deliberately study several archetypes rather than trying to create one mythical “Operator.”

For example:

Control-room / process operator
Monitors continuous or batch processes, responds to alarms, adjusts setpoints, coordinates field personnel, troubleshoots abnormal conditions.

Machine / line operator
Runs a machine or production area, performs changeovers, clears jams, checks quality, replenishes materials, records downtime, performs inspections.

Lead operator / line lead
Coordinates people and equipment, watches production targets, reallocates labor, escalates problems, participates in shift meetings.

Hybrid / highly automated operator
Oversees several assets rather than directly operating one machine; intervention becomes increasingly exception-driven.

Then study their work across a whole shift, not merely while something is wrong.

I’d capture things like:

Dimension	Questions to research
Start of shift	What do they need to know immediately? What happened on the previous shift?
Normal operations	What do they watch? What do they mentally compare?
Abnormal operations	How do they notice something is wrong?
Diagnosis	What systems, people and historical information do they consult?
Intervention	What can they change themselves? What requires approval?
Coordination	Who do they call—maintenance, quality, supervisor, material handler?
Documentation	What must they record, acknowledge, classify or explain?
Meetings	Tier meetings, shift handoff, production meeting, quality review, safety meeting?
Planned work	Changeovers, cleaning, inspections, PMs, sampling, recipe changes
End of shift	What knowledge must be transferred to the next person?

And I would specifically capture workarounds.

Those are incredibly valuable.

“What information do you keep on paper because the system doesn't show it?”

“What spreadsheet do you use?”

“What do you write on the whiteboard?”

“What screen do you leave open all day?”

“What alarm do you ignore?”

“What does an experienced operator know that a new operator doesn't?”

That last question becomes particularly important for AI.

2. Model decisions, not just tasks

This is the piece I’d add to your plan.

Once we understand what operators do, transform the research into a decision inventory.

For example:

Machine speed is dropping.

A task analysis might say:

Operator checks machine status.

But the actual cognitive work may be:

Is this normal variation, an emerging problem, or something requiring immediate intervention?

Then:

What changed?

Is it upstream, here, or downstream?

Has this happened before?

Will it recover itself?

What happens if I do nothing?

What action is safest?

Who else needs to know?

That gives us a much richer design target.

I’d document every important decision using something like:

Signal → Interpretation → Decision → Action → Verification

For example:

Conveyor accumulation rising
↓
Downstream machine appears constrained
↓
Is intervention required?
↓
Slow upstream / inspect downstream / call maintenance
↓
Did accumulation recover?

Now suddenly the role of the interface becomes very clear.

It should help the operator move through that chain.

3. Understand operator modes

I think this could become one of the foundations of the eventual interface.

Operators don't have one information need. Their needs change depending upon what mode they're in.

I’d probably start with something like:

Orient

“What is happening?”

Start of shift. Returning from break. Walking up to a machine. Taking over from another operator.

Monitor

“Is everything okay?”

Normal production.

Investigate

“Something seems wrong. Why?”

Anomaly, alarm, quality deviation, performance drop.

Act

“What should I do?”

Corrective action, setpoint adjustment, procedure, escalation.

Verify

“Did that fix it?”

Watch recovery and unintended consequences.

Coordinate

“Who else needs to know or do something?”

Maintenance, quality, production leadership, material handling.

Handoff

“What does the next person need to know?”

Shift transition, maintenance handoff, escalation.

This matters enormously for AI because the appropriate behavior changes.

During Monitor, AI should probably be quiet.

During Investigate, AI can become much more active.

During Act, AI needs strong controls, explainability and confirmation.

During Handoff, AI could summarize hours of operational history.

That feels much more natural than imagining a permanently open chat window.

4. Map pains and gains

Your instinct here is exactly right, but I'd classify them.

Information pain

“Everything exists, but I have to find it.”

Too many screens. Too many systems. Poor context.

Attention pain

“Everything is asking for my attention.”

Alarm floods are a classic example. Alarm management remains significant enough that IEC 62682 specifically governs alarm systems presented through control systems and HMIs.

Interpretation pain

“I can see the data but don't know what it means.”

Five trends move simultaneously.

Knowledge pain

“I know Joe has seen this problem before.”

Tribal knowledge.

Coordination pain

“I know what needs to happen, but now I have to find someone.”

Execution pain

“The system tells me something is wrong but doesn't help me resolve it.”

Documentation pain

“I just fixed the problem; now I need to spend ten minutes explaining what happened.”

That taxonomy will become useful later because different AI capabilities address different pains.

5. Then ask: what jobs should AI perform?

Rather than starting with “AI assistant”, I'd start with a set of AI roles.

For instance:

Observer
Continuously evaluates system state.

“Filler discharge pressure has been drifting for 18 minutes.”

Filter
Determines what deserves attention.

“There are 17 active alerts, but two appear consequential.”

Interpreter
Explains what the signals collectively mean.

“The throughput loss appears downstream-driven rather than a filler constraint.”

Investigator
Pulls context from multiple systems.

“This pattern occurred three times this month. Each event followed a CIP cycle.”

Advisor
Recommends next steps.

“Inspect valve XV-104 before increasing pump speed.”

Executor
Performs permitted actions.

“Create a maintenance request.”

Coordinator
Communicates with others.

“Maintenance has been notified and the shift lead has been updated.”

Recorder
Documents work automatically.

“I captured the downtime reason and added the intervention to the shift log.”

Teacher
Provides just-in-time guidance.

“Would you like the recovery procedure?”

That is a fundamentally different vision from:

Operator types question → chatbot answers question.

And I think that's where the real “next generation” experience lies.

6. Think carefully about AI initiative

One of the biggest design questions is:

Who starts the interaction?

There are probably four levels.

Operator initiated

“Why is Line 3 slowing down?”

AI suggested

“I noticed an unusual pressure pattern. Want me to investigate?”

AI proactive

“Line 3 is developing a downstream constraint. At the current rate, the buffer will reach capacity in approximately 12 minutes.”

AI autonomous

“I reduced upstream speed 4% to prevent buffer saturation.”

Those are radically different interaction models.

And they require different levels of confidence, risk, authorization and trust.

NIST's research on AI trust is relevant here: user trust depends partly upon the task and the consequence of trusting the system, meaning an AI recommendation for a low-risk informational task shouldn't necessarily be treated the same way as AI controlling an industrial process.

I'd make AI autonomy level one of the explicit dimensions of the research.

Something more like:

Maintenance / CMMS

Then ask:

What if the operator no longer had to understand which system contained the answer?

That could become a major design principle.

The next-generation experience may provide a unified operational context, while AI retrieves information from those underlying systems.

8. Only then define the interface architecture

And here I think things get REALLY fun.

I wouldn't start with:

Dashboard + navigation + AI chat.

I'd explore something more like:

Operational picture

The persistent understanding of what's happening now.

Assets, process, production, current state.

Attention

What needs me?

Anomalies, risks, alarms, upcoming constraints.

Work

What do I need to do?

Tasks, inspections, changeovers, interventions, requested actions.

Investigation

Help me understand this.

Trends, causal relationships, historical comparison, AI reasoning.

Collaboration

Who else is involved?

Maintenance, quality, supervisor, shift team.

And then AI is not one of those areas.

AI is a capability woven through all of them.

That distinction could be extremely important.

Instead of having:

Dashboard | Trends | Alarms | AI

you might have:

Now | Attention | Work | Investigate

with AI operating throughout the whole experience.

9. Explore interaction beyond chat

This is another research area I would definitely include.

Generative AI makes people immediately think chat.

But chat is probably only one interaction mode.

An industrial AI interface might include:

And the UI temporarily assembles the useful trends, alarms, maintenance events and process relationships.

That last one is particularly exciting to me.

The interface itself could become partially generative.

For an operator system, I'd make this a first-class research topic.

Operators will need answers to questions like:

Human oversight is also explicitly emphasized in NIST's AI risk guidance.

And there is one principle I'd keep pinned to the wall

I think the central transition you're exploring may ultimately be something like:

Traditional operator experience

Give me the information so I can figure out what needs my attention.

versus:

Next-generation operator experience

Tell me what deserves my attention, help me understand why, and help me resolve it.

That doesn't mean removing conventional process views, trends, alarms, controls or operator judgment. High-performance HMI thinking has long emphasized making normal versus abnormal conditions easier to perceive, rather than simply making screens visually impressive.

AI lets us take that principle much, much further.

Instead of merely making abnormal conditions perceptually obvious, the system can potentially recognize, contextualize, explain, prioritize, and assist with them.

And that, I think, is where your “next generation Operator” concept starts becoming genuinely different rather than just prettier. 😄

If you're game, I would actually start our next pass at the very beginning and build an Operator Work Model together: a comprehensive map of what an operator does from arriving for a shift through handoff, including routine tasks, abnormal situations, interactions with other roles, meetings, systems, decisions, pains, and unofficial workarounds. That would give us the raw material for everything else.

And I am very glad we didn't skip this step, because Pass 3 is giving us the bridge we wanted: operator work → operator decisions → information requirements → eventual interaction design.

Research Pass 3: Operator Decision & Information Model

The biggest finding is this:

Operators don't primarily consume information. They use information to continuously answer questions.

That sounds obvious, but it has huge interface implications.

A traditional system tends to be organized around data sources and equipment:

Alarms → Trends → Equipment → Production → Quality → Maintenance

The operator's cognitive work is organized around questions:

Are we okay?
What's changing?
Does this matter?
Why is it happening?
What should I do?
What will happen if I do nothing?
Did my action work?

Human-factors research on process operations maps closely to this. Situation awareness is commonly described through three levels: perceiving relevant conditions, comprehending what they mean, and projecting their likely future state. Process interfaces should therefore support all three—not merely expose sensor values.

Manufacturing research points in the same direction from a different angle: shop-floor IT systems have historically underserved workers' process-oriented information needs, creating extra searching, waiting, and communication.

That gives us our foundation.

Part I — The Universal Operator Decision Loop

After the first three research passes, I think we can now make our common work loop more precise:

1. ORIENT

What situation am I responsible for?

2. EVALUATE

Is operation acceptable right now?

3. ANTICIPATE

Where is it heading?

4. TRIAGE

What deserves my attention?

5. DIAGNOSE

What is causing this?

6. SELECT RESPONSE

What should be done?

7. EXECUTE / COORDINATE

Who or what should act?

8. VERIFY

Did the response produce the intended result?

9. PRESERVE CONTEXT

What should be remembered or communicated?

This isn't meant to imply operators consciously walk through nine steps every time.

An expert might collapse:

notice → diagnose → decide

into a two-second recognition.

But these are the underlying decision needs.

And importantly, research on cognitive work analysis in manufacturing explicitly uses approaches such as control-task analysis and the decision ladder to model these kinds of operator cognitive demands and to determine where technology can appropriately assist.

Decision

Consequence of being wrong

Potentially substantial, particularly in process industries.

Shift-handover guidance explicitly emphasizes that incoming staff require more than a raw log: the transfer should meet their information needs, be written and verbal, support clarification, and convey current operating conditions and unusual circumstances.

Current burden

Reconstruction.

The operator may have to reconstruct a meaningful story from disconnected events.

Summarize by significance, not chronology.

Instead of:

06:32 work order
07:03 alarm
07:08 normal

give:

Recurring feed problem remains unresolved. Two resets restored operation temporarily; maintenance inspection is pending.

That is a fundamentally different information product.

Decision 2 — “Are we operating normally?”
EVALUATE

This is the continuous background judgment.

Manufacturing operator evidence

Likely combines:

machine state
cycle/rate
product movement
WIP/accumulation
quality
reject behavior
material availability
sounds/visual cues
planned target
Process operator evidence

Likely combines:

process variables
alarms

control-loop behavior
equipment state
process relationships
quality/lab data
operating envelope
Important distinction

The question usually isn't:

“Is temperature 172°?”

It is:

“Is 172° appropriate given everything else happening?”

That means context changes meaning.

The same value could be:

normal in one product
unusual in another
expected during startup
dangerous during steady-state operation

This also explains why naïve “AI anomaly detection” isn't enough.

Anomaly ≠ problem.

Decision

Can I remain in monitor mode, or do I need to investigate?

AI-value hypothesis ○

Instead of simply detecting statistical deviation:

“Pressure is unusual.”

the system should eventually attempt:

“Pressure is unusual for this operating state, but consistent with the current ramp-up and does not presently require intervention.”

That is much closer to useful decision support.

Decision 3 — “Where is this heading?”
ANTICIPATE

I think this is one of our most important next-generation opportunities.

Research on situation awareness explicitly includes projection of future status as the highest of its three classic levels. Operators need not only perception and comprehension but some ability to anticipate what happens next.

Questions
Is this trend stable?
Is it accelerating?
How much margin remains?
When will it become consequential?
What downstream effect might occur?
Will the system recover?
What happens if no action is taken?
Manufacturing examples

Buffer currently 72% full.

Not very helpful by itself.

More useful:

At the current accumulation rate, the buffer will reach its operating limit in approximately 11 minutes.

Or:

Current production target will likely be missed by 4.5% if micro-stop frequency continues.

Process example

Instead of:

Vessel temperature increasing.

perhaps:

Temperature remains within limits but is rising faster than during comparable batches.

Decision

Should I intervene before a formal limit is reached?

This changes the role of alarms

Alarm systems fundamentally notify operators of conditions requiring timely assessment/action. HSE explicitly says alarms should direct attention toward conditions requiring response and provide enough time for that response before escalation.

A next-generation system could supplement that with weak-signal awareness before an alarm exists.

Interestingly, a 2026 ISA-published book on process situation management explicitly discusses weak signals as a supplement to alarms for operator situation awareness.

That feels very relevant to what we're imagining.

Decision 4 — “What deserves my attention?”
TRIAGE

This is different from merely determining whether something is abnormal.

Operators may simultaneously have:

8 alarms
2 overdue tasks
production below target
maintenance waiting
a quality sample due
an upcoming changeover
one slowly deteriorating machine

The question becomes:

What should I care about first?

Information needed

For every candidate issue:

Severity
How bad is it?

Urgency
How quickly must I act?

Trajectory
Is it worsening?

Impact
Safety? Quality? Production? Equipment?

Recoverability
Will it resolve itself?

Responsibility
Is this actually mine?

Dependencies
Will ignoring it create another problem?

Alarm research strongly supports this general problem. Poor alarm systems can overload operators and degrade situational awareness; alarm management therefore emphasizes relevance, priority, defined response, and human response capability—not simply detection.

Human-in-the-loop simulation has also found that rationalized/state-based alarming can reduce workload and improve process-control performance during abnormal conditions.

Design implication

I think Attention should eventually be much more sophisticated than:

Critical / Warning / Information

It may need something closer to:

ACT NOW
INVESTIGATE
WATCH
NO ACTION

And AI could continuously recalculate that prioritization based on context.

Decision 5 — “What is actually happening?”
DIAGNOSE

This might be the most cognitively expensive operator decision.

Research describes fault diagnosis in process control as a high-level cognitive task requiring multiple inputs and prediction of the outcomes of possible interventions.

The operator needs three types of evidence
A. Current evidence

What is happening now?

signals
states
alarms
product
equipment
physical observations
B. Historical evidence

What led here?

trends
preceding alarms
mode changes
settings
interventions
prior shift actions
C. Comparative evidence

Have we seen something like this before?

previous incidents
normal baseline
similar batch/order
same equipment fault
maintenance history

And perhaps a fourth:

D. Relational evidence

What else changed at the same time?

This matters enormously.

Traditional historian interaction might make the operator manually select:

pressure
flow
valve position
motor current
upstream rate
downstream rate

because an experienced person knows those signals should be compared.

A smarter system might know the functional relationships surrounding the problem.

Research using causal maps in process control found preliminary evidence that adding structured causal information helped participants develop useful understanding of abnormal events without necessarily increasing overall task load.

AI opportunity ○

AI should be able to answer:

“Why do you think that?”

with evidence.

Not:

“I think Pump P102 is failing.”

but:

Pump P102 is one possible source.

Supporting evidence:

discharge pressure falling
motor current rising
inlet pressure stable
valve command unchanged

Alternative:
downstream restriction remains possible.

That produces assisted reasoning rather than outsourced reasoning.

Decision 6 — “What should I do?”
SELECT RESPONSE

Once a likely interpretation exists, operators must choose among possible responses.

Information needed
available actions
procedure
expected result
contraindications
safety constraints
production impact
quality impact
previous successful actions
authority/permissions
whether someone else needs to act
Manufacturing example

Problem:

Repeated label feed failure.

Options might include:

reset
clean sensor
inspect material
reduce speed
change roll
call maintenance
stop production

The best action depends on:

severity + recurrence + operating state + product + previous attempts + risk

Process example

Possible intervention may require predicting how multiple coupled variables respond.

That is precisely why an AI system should be cautious about presenting a single action as the answer.

Better model

Options + rationale + consequences

For example:

Recommended: Reduce line speed 5% and inspect label feed.

Why: Maintains production while lowering feed demand.

Expected: accumulation should stabilize within ~4 minutes.

Alternative: Continue operating and monitor.

Risk: repeated feed faults are currently increasing.

This seems far more useful than:

“Reduce line speed.”

Decision 7 — “Can I trust this recommendation?”

This is technically a new decision introduced by AI.

And it deserves its own place in our model.

The moment AI starts advising rather than simply retrieving information, the operator must evaluate:

Why does it believe this?
How certain is it?
What evidence did it use?
Does it understand my current operating state?
Has this worked before?
What happens if it's wrong?

That means AI creates cognitive work too

We should not assume:

AI assistance = reduced workload.

Poor AI could simply replace:

interpreting process data

with:

interpreting mysterious AI recommendations.

Alarm-management research makes the same broader point: technology only helps when its presentation supports human response capability.

Design hypothesis ○

Any consequential AI recommendation should eventually expose at least:

Recommendation

Why

Evidence

Confidence / uncertainty

Expected consequence

Ability to inspect further

Not necessarily all visible simultaneously—but accessible.

Decision dimensions

The operator isn't merely choosing a technical action.

They're choosing a response pathway.

Decision 9 — “Did it work?”
VERIFY

This is surprisingly important.

Human beings are vulnerable to what we might loosely call premature closure:

“I did something. The alarm cleared. Done.”

But those aren't necessarily equivalent.

Verification requires knowing:

What should change?

How quickly?

What else should remain stable?

What constitutes recovery?

Example:

Operator reduces speed.

Success isn't:

button accepted.

It might be:

micro-stop rate falls
accumulation stabilizes
output becomes consistent
reject rate remains acceptable

AI opportunity ○

Before execution:

Expected recovery signals

After execution:

✓ Flow stabilized
✓ Buffer decreasing
✓ Quality unchanged
○ Monitoring recurrence for 10 min

Now the interface closes the decision loop.

Decision 10 — “What should become shared knowledge?”
PRESERVE CONTEXT

Once the immediate work is complete:

Is this worth recording?
Does maintenance need it?
Does the next shift need it?
Does supervision need it?
Does this represent a recurring pattern?

That means documentation isn't merely administration.

It's part of organizational memory.

And the underlying value of good digital assistance may be partly its ability to externalize knowledge that otherwise remains in individual workers' heads. Manufacturing research describes assistance systems as cognitive aids that can reduce memory demands and preserve process knowledge, while also warning that rigid assistance can reduce operator flexibility.

That balance is important.

A second major finding: information has different roles

I think we should stop classifying operator information only by source.

Instead, classify it by what it does for the decision.

STATUS

What is true now?

Example: Line speed = 118/min.

BASELINE

What should I expect?

Example: Normal = 118–125/min.

CHANGE

What changed?

Example: Rate began falling 14 minutes ago.

RELATIONSHIP

What else is connected?

Example: Rate fell as downstream accumulation increased.

HISTORY

Has this happened before?

Example: Similar pattern occurred three times this week.

CONSEQUENCE

Why should I care?

Example: At current trajectory, target will be missed.

OPTIONS

What can I do?

Example: reduce upstream speed / inspect labeler / continue monitoring.

GUIDANCE

How do I safely do it?

Procedure/instructions.

FEEDBACK

Did it work?

Recovery indicators.

This taxonomy could become incredibly useful when we start deciding what belongs on screen at any moment.

Instead of putting everything everywhere, we ask:

What role does this information play in the current decision?

And that gives us an “information ladder”

↓

Traditional operational systems are excellent at the bottom of that ladder:

A next-generation operator experience could help move the human toward:

while preserving access downward to the evidence.

That may be one of the clearest descriptions we've found yet of the design opportunity.

But manufacturing gives us an important warning

We shouldn't assume that “more assistance” is always better.

Research into intelligent manufacturing assistance systems has found trade-offs: such systems can improve feedback and information processing while potentially reducing workers' autonomy over scheduling, methods, or decisions.

Similarly, user-centered research on manufacturing guidance systems has emphasized simple systems and voluntary use rather than assuming workers always want maximal intervention.

I think that's incredibly relevant for AI.

Another principle for our wall:

Assist the operator's judgment; don't unnecessarily confiscate it.

An expert operator saying:

“Get out of my way—I know what's happening”

should probably be a supported interaction mode.

The Decision-Risk Matrix

Before we ever decide how autonomous AI should be, I think every operator decision should eventually receive three ratings:

URGENCY

How quickly must this decision happen?

CONSEQUENCE

How costly is a wrong decision?

REVERSIBILITY

How easily can the action be undone?

That gives us very different AI behaviors.

For example:

Decision	Urgency	Consequence	Reversibility	Potential AI posture
Which trend to show	Low	Low	High	Do automatically
Prioritize likely problem	Medium	Medium	High	Proactively suggest
Draft maintenance ticket	Low	Low	High	Prepare automatically
Reduce line speed slightly	Medium	Medium	High-ish	Recommend / confirm
Change critical process setpoint	High	High	Variable	Strong human control
Safety shutdown	High	Very high	Low	Governed deterministic system/procedure

This is going to stop us from falling into the trap of:

“Should AI be autonomous?”

That's the wrong question.

The right question is:

For which decisions, under which conditions, should AI have which level of initiative and authority?

Much better.

Research Pass 4: Human–Automation & Human–AI Interaction
1. The goal is not “trust the AI”

This is probably the most important finding.

Human–automation research distinguishes between appropriate/calibrated trust and simply maximizing trust. Too much trust leads to inappropriate reliance; too little means useful automation gets ignored.

So we should not design the operator experience around:

“How do we make operators trust AI?”

We should design around:

“How do we help operators know when AI deserves reliance—and when it doesn't?”

That is a much healthier design goal.

NIST's current Industrial AI work makes almost exactly this point: industrial AI needs methods for establishing appropriate levels of trust, communicating performance expectations, and making its logic and reasoning understandable to users.

And NIST's new 2026 AI-for-Manufacturing initiative specifically calls out human-AI collaboration effectiveness, operator understanding, interpretability, traceability, and trust as measurement targets.

Principle 1
Calibrate trust; don't manufacture confidence.

Which means the interface needs to let the operator recognize:

AI knows this well.

AI isn't sure about this.

AI doesn't have enough information.

Those are all successful AI behaviors.

2. AI advice creates a new operator task: evaluating the AI

We touched this in Pass 3, but the research makes it much more important.

The moment the AI says:

“I believe Pump P102 is degrading.”

the operator's job becomes:

“Should I believe you?”

Human-AI systems can suffer from automation bias: people may accept automated recommendations even when those recommendations are wrong, particularly under workload, complexity, or time pressure. A systematic review found that trust, task complexity, time pressure, user confidence and experience all influence susceptibility.

So merely putting this next to the recommendation:

Confidence: 87%

doesn't solve the problem.

We need to support inspection.

Instead of:

🔴 Bearing failure likely
87% confidence

I'd prefer something like:

Possible bearing degradation

Why this surfaced:

motor vibration increasing
current rising
output falling
pattern differs from normal product-change behavior

AI assessment: moderate confidence

Compare previous events · See evidence

The operator can interrogate the claim.

Principle 2
AI recommendations should be inspectable, not authoritative pronouncements.
3. Explainability does NOT mean showing an AI essay

This one made me smile because it validates something we've already been circling.

Research into explainable recommendations shows that explanation can improve trust calibration—but poorly designed explanations can actually cause people to accept or reject recommendations less thoughtfully. Researchers have proposed designing explanations for active engagement, attention guidance and even intentional friction when appropriate.

So we should not interpret “explainable AI” as:

AI generates six paragraphs explaining itself.

For an operator, explanation probably needs layers.

Level 1 — Meaning

Filler instability developing

Level 2 — Why

Pressure oscillation + valve cycling are increasing together.

Level 3 — Evidence

Actual signals, trends, events.

Level 4 — Model/context

Compared against previous 30 minutes and similar production runs.

That gives us progressive disclosure.

An expert might stop at Level 1.

A novice might continue to Level 4.

Principle 3
Explain on demand, with evidence underneath the explanation.

And importantly:

“Why?” may become one of the most important interactions in the entire product.

4. AI should earn the right to interrupt

This is one I'm especially interested in.

Traditional operator systems often treat notifications independently.

Alarm happens:

BEEP.

Task overdue:

notification.

Maintenance message:

notification.

AI detects something:

notification.

Pretty soon we have simply invented another alarm source.

And we already know what happens when systems overproduce demands on operator attention. Alarm floods degrade situational awareness; experimental work with control-room operators has shown that better alarm presentation and response strategies can improve performance.

So our AI cannot behave like an enthusiastic coworker standing beside the operator saying:

“Hey Amy! I noticed something!”

every few minutes.

😂

Instead we need an initiative policy.

I'd start with four levels.

0 — Silent

AI observes but does not interrupt.

Normal operation.

1 — Ambient

AI changes the presentation without demanding attention.

Minor deviation highlighted.

2 — Proactive

AI explicitly surfaces something.

“Label feed instability is increasing.”

3 — Interruptive

AI demands operator attention.

“Tank level is approaching an operating constraint. Action required.”

And the jump between levels should depend on things like:

Urgency × consequence × trajectory × confidence × operator context

Not simply:

anomaly detected = interrupt.

Principle 4
The cost of interruption should be part of every AI decision.

That one belongs on our wall.

5. Sometimes the best AI interaction is no interaction

This follows directly.

During healthy operation, the operator's job includes maintaining awareness.

If AI continually says:

Everything looks good!

Everything still looks good!

Good news—the pump is normal!

we haven't assisted them.

We've created cognitive noise.

This suggests a behavioral rule:

The better normal operation is going, the quieter the intelligence layer should become.

Think of a competent human teammate.

You don't want someone beside you narrating every observation.

You want them to speak when:

you missed something
something materially changed
they possess information relevant to your decision
they need your action

That is a much better model for AI.

6. Automation can actually make operators worse at intervention

This is our first major caution against simply automating as much cognitive work as possible.

Decades of human-automation research describe the out-of-the-loop performance problem.

As automation takes over more activity, human operators can lose situation awareness or become less prepared to diagnose and recover when automation behaves unexpectedly.

This is particularly relevant to our operator concept.

Imagine AI eventually does all of this:

Detect problem
Diagnose problem
Choose response
Execute response
Verify recovery

The operator may appear incredibly productive.

Until the AI is wrong.

Then suddenly we ask the human:

“Your turn! Fix the unusual condition you haven't been actively reasoning about for the last four hours.”

That's precisely the situation we want to avoid.

NASA's current human-system standard offers a useful analogue from another safety-critical domain: automation should be designed around defined human roles, the human should generally retain ultimate authority, responsibilities should optimize the performance of the integrated human/automation team, and operators need to understand the basis and consequences of automated recommendations.

Principle 5
AI should reduce unnecessary cognitive labor without removing necessary operational understanding.

That's subtly different from:

automate everything possible.

Different universe.

Principle 6
Authority follows risk, not AI capability.

Just because AI can execute something doesn't mean it should.

10. Uncertainty must be a legitimate interface state

AI systems will encounter:

missing sensors
bad data
unfamiliar operating states
conflicting evidence
insufficient historical examples
model drift
situations outside training/validation conditions

The worst behavior would be confidently improvising.

The interface needs ways of saying:

I don't have enough evidence.

or:

This operating condition is outside the range where this model has been validated.

The 2026 NIST critical-infrastructure work specifically discusses systems being monitored for operation outside verified validity regions and degrading gracefully and transparently when adverse conditions occur.

Principle 7
Uncertainty should change AI behavior, not merely change a confidence number.

Meaning:

High confidence + low risk:

“Here's what I found.”

Moderate confidence:

“Here's the likely explanation and alternatives.”

Low confidence:

“I can't determine the cause yet. These are the most useful things to inspect next.”

That last answer is still extremely useful.

11. AI should distinguish observation from inference

This feels crucial for industrial environments.

Suppose the interface says:

Bearing degradation detected.

What does that mean?

Was there actually a bearing sensor detecting degradation?

Or did AI infer degradation from vibration, temperature and current?

Those need to look different.

I would establish three semantic categories:

OBSERVED

Motor current increased 18%.

Measured fact.

DERIVED

Current exceeds its normal range for this production state.

Calculation/context.

INFERRED

Bearing degradation is a plausible explanation.

AI interpretation.

That separation would help the operator understand what is known versus what is believed.

Principle 8
Never visually blur measured fact and AI inference.

I think this could become one of our most important UI rules.

Principle 9
Make assistance expandable, not unavoidable.
13. AI needs a shared mental model with the operator

Human-AI teaming literature repeatedly identifies a shared mental model and effective communication as important to successful collaboration.

For example:

Motor current increased 14%.

Derived

Motor current is outside its normal operating range for Product A.

AI interpretation

Feed roller degradation may be contributing.

Different semantic treatment.

Perhaps even visually different typography/icons.

The goal is:

Never let inference masquerade as instrumentation.

12. DECISION SUPPORT
“What can I do?”

Once the operator understands the situation, the interface changes from explanation to decision support.

Perhaps:

Recommended response

Inspect label feed path and roller before restarting at full rate.

Why?

Recurrence after reset suggests the interruption is not transient.

Alternatives

Continue at reduced speed

Production continues, but recurrence remains likely.

Request maintenance inspection

Higher downtime now; may prevent repeated stops.

The operator can inspect:

Expected outcome

Risk

Previous results

This supports judgment rather than replacing it.

13. Action should occur in context

Suppose the operator selects:

Request maintenance

The interface should not dump them into another application.

Instead:

Maintenance request prepared

Asset: Labeler 2
Issue: recurring feed interruption
Started: 10:19
Production impact: −7% current rate
Operator actions: reset at 10:31
Evidence: attached
Similar event: Aug 19

Operator can add:

“I can hear intermittent chatter near the feed roller.”

Then:

Send request

Now we've captured human observation alongside machine data.

That's exactly the combined intelligence model we've been researching.

14. WORK
“What am I responsible for accomplishing?”

The Work space handles intentional work and actions generated by situations.

This might include:

NOW

Inspect Labeler 2 feed roller
Generated from active situation

UPCOMING

Quality sample — due 11:10

Packaging replenishment — approximately 11:25

LATER

Product changeover — scheduled 13:00

Notice what is happening:

Traditional application:

task list

Next-generation environment:

context-aware operating plan

If an urgent problem occurs, the system understands that priorities changed.

15. Work should understand dependencies

Changeover, for example, might appear as:

Change to Product B — 13:00

✓ Product available
✓ Labels staged
✓ Tooling ready
⚠ Quality release pending
○ Run remaining Product A
○ Stop line
○ Clean contact surfaces
○ Change tooling
○ Load recipe
○ First-piece verification

Now AI can assist without “taking over.”

For example:

Quality release is still pending. At the current production rate, this may delay changeover by approximately 12 minutes.

That's useful.

Not:

“Hello! Would you like some help with your changeover today?”

😂

16. VERIFY becomes an explicit interaction

This is a design element I particularly like.

Suppose the operator takes an action.

The situation doesn't immediately disappear.

Instead it enters:

Recovery monitoring

Intervention: Reduced line speed 5%

Expected:

feed interruptions decrease
buffer stabilizes
reject rate remains normal

Then the interface watches.

A few minutes later:

Recovering

✓ No new feed interruption — 7 min
✓ accumulation stable
✓ quality normal
○ monitoring another 3 min

Then:

Resolved

Operating state stable.

And the situation collapses into operational memory.

That closes the cognitive loop.

17. Handoff becomes almost automatic

Now imagine the operator reaches end of shift.

Because we've been capturing situations, actions and work all day, the handoff shouldn't require reconstructing everything.

The system prepares:

Shift handoff
Needs attention next shift

Labeler feed issue — monitor recurrence

Maintenance inspected roller at 14:20. No recurrence for 2.1 hrs.

Production

97.4% attainment
Primary loss: labeler interruptions

Quality

No unresolved deviations.

Maintenance

Feed roller inspection completed. Replacement deferred.

Upcoming

Product C change scheduled 19:30.

And the operator can say:

Add: “If it starts chattering again, call maintenance before resetting it repeatedly.”

Beautiful.

That's human knowledge becoming persistent operational context.

18. Operational Memory

This starts to deserve recognition as an actual product capability.

Every meaningful situation becomes a reusable record:

What happened

What evidence existed

What people thought

What was tried

What worked

What did not

Who participated

What ultimately resolved it

Future AI can then use these records:

“This looks similar to the August 26 incident.”

And the operator can inspect that incident.

That's how tribal knowledge slowly becomes organizational knowledge.

19. What about conventional HMI controls?

They're still needed.

Very much so.

But they become contextual tools, not necessarily the organizing architecture.

Operators still need:

faceplates
setpoints
process graphics
trends
alarms
permissives/interlocks
modes
sequences

For process/control-room environments especially, conventional process visualization remains absolutely essential.

I would expose those as part of the Now and Investigate experiences.

For example:

click pump → equipment detail

with:

state
controls
related process context
history
active situations

rather than maintaining a complete conceptual separation between:

HMI world

and

AI world.

21. The persistent AI affordance

I think there should be a way to access AI globally.

But I would make it small and contextual, not a permanent 35%-width chatbot panel.

Perhaps:

Ask

or a compact input:

Ask about Line 2…

And what the user asks determines what happens.

“Why are we behind target?”

→ opens an investigation.

“Show Labeler 2.”

→ navigates operational context.

“When is the next changeover?”

→ surfaces Work.

“What happened last shift?”

→ operational memory.

“Create a maintenance request for this.”

→ action workflow.

This is less like chat.

More like natural-language access to the entire operational environment.

22. Proactive AI should appear where the work is

This is another important decision.

If AI detects something relevant to production:

It appears in Now and perhaps Attention.

If AI discovers a conflict with a future task:

It appears in Work.

If AI discovers supporting evidence during investigation:

It appears in Investigate.

We don't funnel every intelligent event through:

🤖 AI Assistant

That would create an artificial conceptual boundary.

23. AI initiative ladder

I'd formally make this part of the UX framework:

OBSERVE

No visible interaction.

ENRICH

Add context to existing UI.

Example:

“Typical range 115–126”

SURFACE

Bring something to awareness.

“Rate deterioration detected.”

ENGAGE

Offer contextual assistance.

“Investigate”

RECOMMEND

Propose action.

PREPARE

Stage action/workflow.

EXECUTE

Only where permitted by policy/risk.

The UI could potentially communicate this implicitly rather than showing the vocabulary to users, but internally it gives us an excellent design framework.

27. The design principles I would officially carry forward

I think we've now earned a first real design manifesto.

1. Design around operator decisions, not software modules.
2. Normal operation should be quiet.
3. Surface situations, not floods of events.
4. Show trajectory, not merely current state.
5. Bring evidence to the operator instead of sending the operator searching.
6. Keep facts, derived information and AI inference distinguishable.
7. Make AI reasoning inspectable.
8. Assistance should deepen on demand.
9. AI initiative must scale with urgency, confidence and consequence.
10. Authority must scale with risk.
11. Actions remain attached to the context that caused them.
12. Verification is part of the action—not an afterthought.
13. Operational knowledge should accumulate over time.
14. AI spans the interface; it is not a destination.
15. Preserve operator understanding while reducing unnecessary cognitive labor.

That is enough to start evaluating virtually any feature idea.

And we've reached our first genuinely major design decision

This is where I'm going to stop making decisions for you.

Because I think there are two legitimate product directions, and the choice affects almost everything downstream.

Direction A — Evolutionary

The primary experience remains recognizably an industrial HMI/operator interface.

The operational/process visualization is the center of gravity.

Now is essentially the evolution of the HMI.

Attention, Work, Investigation and AI intelligence surround and enhance it.

Conceptually:

The HMI becomes intelligent.

This would probably feel safer, more familiar, and easier to introduce into existing automation environments.

Direction B — Transformational

The primary experience becomes an operator workspace.

The system is organized around:

Now
Attention
Work
Investigate

and process graphics/HMI controls are tools inside that environment when needed.

The dominant unit isn't:

screen

or even:

asset

It increasingly becomes:

situation + work + operating context

Conceptually:

The operator gets a new operating system for their work.

This direction is much more ambitious.

And I suspect it has considerably more potential to feel genuinely next-generation.

But it also asks users—and your product organization—to make a larger conceptual leap.

And that is the first choice I don't want to quietly make while you're out taking your well-earned walk. 😄

Everything before it I feel comfortable choosing based on the research.

But “Are we evolving the HMI, or redefining the operator workspace?” is a product-strategy decision masquerading as a UX decision.

So I think this is the perfect stopping point.

Go give your brain something completely unrelated to industrial operations. 😂 When you come back with fresh eyes, you don't need to digest every word above again. Start with just those final two directions.

Because your instinctive reaction to A versus B will tell us an awful lot about where we should take the interface next.
